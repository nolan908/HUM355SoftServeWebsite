The Cone as It Appears

Contemporary fast-food soft serve is more than a consumer product: it constitutes an infrastructure of alienation in the fast-food industry. We take an interest in how McDonald's operates. Infrastructure, in this context, refers to foundational systems such as production, distribution, and technological platforms, each of which enables the creation and delivery of soft serve. Meanwhile, alienation entails the separation of workers from the products, processes, and outcomes of their labor.

The vanilla soft serve cone handed to a customer is embedded in an extraction network, a system that extends across dairy production, industrial processing, refrigeration, transportation, franchise labor, machine design, maintenance, and consumer standards, where each element plays a role in the supply chain. Together, we see how the different sections throughout this article show that alienation is embedded and obscured within fast-food infrastructures.

This analysis examines late twentieth and twenty first century fast-food supply chains, offering a distinct approach: rather than tracing alienation to global supply chains, it draws on Marx’s theory of alienated labor1. According to Marx, alienation manifests when workers are separated from the product, process, and value of their labor under capitalism, regardless of era or scale. Modern fast-food supply chains intensify and obscure alienation, dispersing production while presenting the finished product as detached from the workers, animals, materials, and machines involved.

🥛

Ingredient Origins

This complexity is seen in a clear example. A McDonald’s vanilla cone is not simply milk from a cow. The ingredient list for McDonald’s vanilla reduced fat ice cream includes many items: milk, sugar, cream, corn syrup, natural flavor, mono and diglycerides, cellulose gum, guar gum, carrageenan, and vitamin A palmitate2. McDonald's USA details these ingredients on its official nutrition and ingredients page2.

This reveals how layered the product is. Each ingredient links the restaurant to dairy farms, sugar and corn production, stabilizer manufacturing, food science, and distribution. Efficiency is defined by speed, uniformity, food safety, low cost, standardization, and managerial control. Conversely, worker autonomy and transparency are deprioritized.

⚙️

Industrial Processing

Ice Cream Manufacturing: Principles and Methods Explained (2024) describes the industrial process3. It includes mixing, pasteurization, homogenization, aging, and freezing. Each stage is shaped by food science. The mixture is then transported and stored under refrigeration. Individual effort is distributed across a global system of logistics and machinery. The finished cone, seen by the consumer as ordinary, is actually the endpoint of a system that hides its origins, labor, and supply chain complexity. This example shows how alienation is embedded in fast-food commodities, as Marx argued. Each step, processing, transport, and technology, conceals the links between labor, materials, and consumption.

🚲 ❄️

Cold Chain Logistics

The cold chain is critical. The USDA defines cold-chain food flows as temperature-controlled delivery from producer to consumer4. This process is vital for food safety and quality. Scholars like Mayhew and Quinlan (2002) argue that standardization and control in fast-food logistics enable efficiency but also obscure the labor that goes into the product5. The last stage in the soft-serve cone's creation shows the alienation built into fast-food infrastructure. At every stage, workers are isolated from the final product.

👷

Fragmented Labor

Marxist theory clarifies these forces. In Estranged Labour, Marx writes, "the worker becomes a servant of his object." Labor produces objects and values that control workers rather than belong to them. This is central to fast-food soft serve. Workers dispense cones, clean machines, serve customers, and address complaints, but do not control products, machines, contracts, prices, recipes, or sales value. Their labor completes the commodity, yet ownership and control stay with the company and franchise. The worker’s link to the product is indirect, mediated by capital. Theory frames this analysis of alienation throughout the supply chain.

Alienation takes root long before the soft-serve cone reaches a customer. For Marx, alienation is the estrangement of workers from their products, processes, and the value of their labor under capitalism, frequently fueled by specialization and external control. Across the chain, dairy workers, processors, drivers, warehouse operators, restaurant staff, and technicians each play a role. Yet their labor remains fragmented; each person handles just one piece of the process. The product’s standardization erases the makers’ identities.

⚙️ 🔓

The Machine as Authority

Marx argues that alienation happens in production. He calls this process active alienation. The supply chain does not create alienation itself, but extends labor organization for capital’s benefit. By combining ingredients, transport, corporate rules, maintenance, and machinery outside worker control, the supply chain intensifies alienation. Under capitalism, machines are introduced to extract surplus value and cut costs, not to reduce labor. The soft-serve machine illustrates this in restaurants. Predefined recipes, cleaning cycles, and digital interfaces define strict parameters for workers, reinforcing their alienation.

The Taylor C602 soft-serve manual stresses the machine's significance as infrastructure6. Through heat-treatment cycles, cleaning requirements, temperature controls, and lockout conditions, the machine can prevent operation if protocols are not followed. The manual specifies that the heat-treatment cycle must be completed at least once every 24 hours. If not, the freezer automatically enters standby mode. These technical requirements carry philosophical consequences. The machine organizes workers’ labor rather than simply assisting them. Workers must respond to programmed cycles, error messages, and lockouts.

Assemblage & Politics

Langdon Winner’s analysis is instructive in this context. In Do Artifacts Have Politics?, Winner contends that machines and systems should be evaluated based on how they embody power and authority, not solely on efficiency7. Artifacts are constructed, maintained, and used within social systems. Their designs are able to reinforce political arrangements. The soft-serve machine determines who may access, repair, clean, or override the system. Thus, it represents power relations. While some authority is vested in workers, greater control resides with corporate policies, service technicians, health codes, and managers. In this partnership, franchises bear the heavy cost of these repairs while McDonald's benefits a larger corporation, Taylor, rather than the consumer. This proprietary arrangement discourages innovation and makes it difficult to repair the machines through lockouts via verification codes. Consequently, all roads lead to 'call the guy,' allowing Taylor to generate 25% of its business revenue strictly from repairs, a case study in proprietary control documented in Johnny Harris’s investigative reporting on the machine’s proprietary repair lockouts8. McDonald's further reinforces this by locking franchises out of any other equipment deals, illustrating how the machine's technical lockout is a tool of economic and political authority.

Winner’s framework indicates that the politics of the soft-serve machine are fundamentally hierarchical. These machines achieve uniformity and safety by limiting worker discretion. Jane Bennett’s assemblage theory, which examines how diverse human and nonhuman components join to form complex networks, provides additional insight9. Bennett defines an assemblage as a combination of human and nonhuman elements that collectively produce outcomes, noting that agency emanates from the interactions among multiple entities rather than from any single actant.

Within this system, the soft-serve cone is understood as an assemblage comprising cows, feed, milk, sugar, freezers, refrigerants, trucks, electricity, workers, franchise rules, cleaning chemicals, temperature sensors, menus, and consumer desire. Bennett’s perspective resists reductionism by highlighting that the cone is the product of a network rather than a single agent. Capitalist ownership and class-based value extraction ultimately determine the conditions of labor and alienation.

Hidden Flows

Maria Kaika’s City of Flows further strengthens this analysis10. Kaika contends that contemporary infrastructure creates the illusion that natural and social processes are separate, despite their deep interconnection. In her examination of water, the faucet presents water as immediate, clean, and natural, while concealing the dams, pipes, labor, capital, and political decisions required to deliver it to the home. Soft-serve is extracted, processed, stabilized, refrigerated, branded, and dispensed. This makes soft serve an exemplary case of how infrastructure conceals the social labor embedded within a commodity. Kaika also argues that the modern home appears independent from natural and social processes only because the flows of nature, social relations, and money that support it are hidden, fetishized, or visually severed11. This is exactly how fast-food soft serve looks. The customer does not see the cow, the dairy worker, the processing plant, the refrigerated truck, or the cleaning cycle. The customer sees only the counter, the employee, and the machine. The system's invisibility makes the commodity feel immediate and natural. According to Emeka W. Dumbili, the influence of the McDonaldization process extends across modern society, so when the machine breaks down, the normally invisible system suddenly becomes apparent by its very absence11.

Breakdown Moment

According to the United States Environmental Protection Agency's Proceedings Eighth National Symposium on Food Processing Wastes, operational issues can prevent a cone from being served, making customers aware of the complexity behind what seems to be a simple product12. This interruption draws attention to the connection between workers' experiences and consumers' perceptions of complex food supply systems. The worker is alienated from the product because they do not control the process or the value it creates. The consumer is alienated in different ways: they are separated from the labor, materials, and infrastructure embedded in the commodity. The worker knows more about the machine’s difficulty than the consumer does, but even the worker does not control the larger system. When the machine is “broken,” both forms of alienation meet. The consumer’s desire confronts the worker’s limited power. The worker becomes the visible representative of an invisible system. ("Estranged Labour", Karl Marx)

Mapping the Infrastructure

At this stage, the philosophical analysis becomes tangible. The purpose is not simply to assert that supply chains are problematic or that soft-serve is unethical, but to map the hidden infrastructure underlying an ordinary commodity and to make visible how alienation is materially produced at each stage. The visual map functions as both an analytical and an illustrative tool, a detailed, multi-layered diagram that clarifies the connections and processes that are typically obscured in the supply chain. This map takes the form of a flowchart or network diagram, with each node denoting a distinct step or actor in the production and delivery of soft-serve, and directional arrows or connectors indicating the movement of materials, labor, and information. The map is divided into four interconnected sections. The first section depicts foundational agricultural and animal systems through pictorial symbols and labeled icons, such as stylized images of dairy cows, sugarcane, and chemical drums, to indicate the sourcing of milk, sugar, stabilizers, and flavoring. The second section traces these raw materials through a sequence of processing and logistics stages, with each processing step—pasteurization, homogenization, mixing, industrial aging, and refrigerated storage—represented by distinct icons or labeled boxes, linked by arrows to refrigerated trucks and storage facilities, illustrating the cold-chain required for safe transport to the restaurant. The third section focuses on in-restaurant flows and routines, depicting freezer storage with cold symbol icons, the soft-serve machine and its critical cycles, worker tasks broken down into cleaning, machine operation, and customer service, and incorporating thermometer and clock visuals to represent regulatory and timing requirements. Flow arrows or color-coded pathways clarify how machine protocols direct and constrain worker autonomy at each stage. The fourth section visually highlights system breakdowns, including warning triangles for lockouts and error notifications, gears or tools for repair procedures, expressive icons for customer frustration, silhouettes of managers for shifting responsibility, and a corporate building symbolizing escalation to upper management. By sequencing and spatially arranging these components, the map delivers a concrete, stepwise visualization of both material and control flows that are otherwise hidden within the fast-food network. This specificity makes certain that viewers can trace how alienation is embedded and reproduced throughout the system, and shows that when the soft-serve machine is “broken,” it is not only a technical issue but also a moment that exposes the normally invisible infrastructures and relationships underpinning the commodity.

The visual map therefore does not only show where soft-serve comes from. It shows how control is distributed unevenly across the system. The worker appears at the final point of contact, but the worker does not control the farm, factory, truck, recipe, machine design, repair system, or corporate policy. The consumer appears at the endpoint of the chain, but their experience depends on hidden labor and machinery. The machine sits between them, translating a complex supply chain into a simple act of service when it works and a visible failure when it does not. The map makes this relation visible by connecting the cone to the wider network that normally disappears behind it.

Conclusion

Accordingly, this analysis transitions from an abstract discussion of international supply chains to a focused examination of a single commodity. Soft-serve is particularly instructive because of its ordinariness, which masks the infrastructure underlying its production. Marx demonstrates that labor becomes alienated when workers generate value they do not own. Winner shows that machines and technical systems can embody authority. Bennett reveals that the commodity emerges from an assemblage of human and nonhuman forces. Kaika explains that contemporary infrastructure obscures the production of nature by making flows appear natural, clean, and immediate. Collectively, these theorists provide a greater understanding of fast-food soft serve as more than a dessert; it is a small, frozen manifestation of capitalist infrastructure.

To summarize, fast-food soft-serve demonstrates how contemporary infrastructure intensifies the separation between workers, commodities, and economic power. The cone is perceived as a simple object of consumption, yet it represents the endpoint of a network involving extraction, processing, refrigeration, machinery, maintenance, and labor. The worker who hands the cone to the customer occupies the visible edge of a much larger, invisible system. Mapping this system makes alienation more apparent, revealing that the commodity is not inherently detached from human origins but is constructed to appear so. The essential task is to reverse this appearance by tracing the flows that capitalism conceals.
